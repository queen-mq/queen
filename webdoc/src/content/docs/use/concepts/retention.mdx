Consuming a message does not delete it, and by default nothing else does either: a queue
has no retention until you configure it. The deliberate default for a store whose replay
window is its retained data, and it means an unattended queue grows until you decide
otherwise.

## Configuring it

```json
{
  "queue": "orders.created",
  "options": {
    "retentionEnabled": true,
    "retentionSeconds": 604800,
    "completedRetentionSeconds": 3600,
    "maxWaitTimeSeconds": 0,
    "dedupWindowSeconds": 3600,
    "leaseTime": 300,
    "retryLimit": 3
  }
}
```

`/configure` is a [full replace](/reference/queue-options): every option you omit,
`retentionEnabled` included, is reset to its default, so re-send the complete configuration
whenever you change one value.

A queue created by a push, or configured without retention keys, carries
`retentionEnabled: false` and all three windows at zero; segments then accumulate for as
long as PostgreSQL has room. The options are evaluated in the background, partition by
partition, as small bounded deletes; cadence and tuning are on [Self-hosting](/selfhost).

## The two consumption-aware rules

Both require `retentionEnabled: true` *and* a positive window. A window without the flag does
nothing, and the flag with both windows at zero deletes nothing.

| Option | Deletes | Bounded by consumption |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `retentionSeconds` | Segments older than the window | No (deletes whether or not anyone consumed them) |
| `completedRetentionSeconds` | Segments older than the window | Yes (never past the slowest group's cursor) |

`retentionSeconds` deletes on age alone: a consumer down longer than the window comes back
to a shorter log. `completedRetentionSeconds` is the safe one: its boundary is capped at the
*lowest* `committed` cursor across every group on the partition, plus one. So unconsumed
backlog is never deleted by it, a partition no group has ever popped is never touched by it,
and one lagging or abandoned group holds data for every other group: deleting a group you no
longer use releases its floor. When both rules apply, the further boundary wins.

## Deletion is whole-segment, so windows are approximate

Messages are stored in segments: one row holding many frames, compressed together, with one
commit timestamp for the whole row. Retention only deletes segments **entirely** below the
computed boundary, so a straddling segment survives with everything in it. Effective
per-message retention is your window plus up to the age span of its segment: a retention
window is a floor on what is kept, not a ceiling on what is deleted. The consumption cap of
`completedRetentionSeconds` is an offset and can fall mid-segment; the covering segment then
stays, because its unconsumed tail is still needed.

## maxWaitTimeSeconds deletes segments on age alone, in-flight leases included

It deletes whole segments older than its cutoff **for every consumer group, consumed or
not, currently leased to a running worker or not**. The SQL that implements it documents
this as data loss by design. It applies regardless of `retentionEnabled`, dead-letters
nothing (aged-out messages are gone, not quarantined), and leaves cursors below the new
watermark to resume at the next offset that still exists.

Use it when staleness itself makes a message worthless: a market tick, a sensor sample, a
cache invalidation. **Do not use it as a backstop for slow consumers: it silently removes
work a worker is holding a lease on.**

## The dead-letter queue is never purged

Retention never touches `queen.log_dlq`. Dead-letter rows survive every rule above and leave
only by per-message delete, replay, or deleting the whole queue, which makes the dead-letter
queue trustworthy for debugging and means an unattended one grows without bound. See
[Leases, retries and the dead-letter queue](/use/concepts/leases-retries-dlq).

## Empty partitions are removed after 30 days; queues never are

Retention advances a per-partition watermark and deletes segments below it. The partition
row itself stays, offsets never reused and never renumbered, for as long as it holds
anything or is being used. A separate cleanup phase deletes partitions **empty and untouched
for `PARTITION_CLEANUP_DAYS`**, 30 days by default: no push and no consumer-group activity
in the window, and no segments, dead-letter rows, streaming-query state or in-flight lease.
Any one of those keeps the partition alive indefinitely.

Deleting the partition drops the consumer cursors with it. That costs a group its
`total_consumed` counter, not its position: there is nothing left to re-read, and a group's
subscription record is per queue, so a later push that recreates the partition seeds it like
a brand-new one. Do not assume offset continuity: a recreated partition is a new partition,
with a new id, starting at offset 0. Set `QUEEN_PARTITION_CLEANUP_ENABLED=false` to keep
every partition row forever, the behaviour of versions before this one. Removing a queue is
always an explicit administrative action.

## Retention is the replay window

Every position lookup (a timestamp seek, a timestamp subscription, the boundary walk
itself) starts at the partition's watermark:

- A timestamp older than the oldest retained segment resolves to that oldest segment, with
  no error to tell you the rest is gone.
- A dead-letter hand-off needs the segment covering the poison message. If retention
  already removed it, the ack comes back `dlq rejected` and the cursor does not advance.
- Sizing the retention window is sizing how far back an incident can be replayed. Decide it
  from the recovery you want, not from disk alone.

Both position mechanisms are on [Replay and subscription modes](/use/concepts/replay).

## The ack window is separate

Transaction ids resolve to offsets through a hash sidecar with its **own** expiry,
`max(dedupWindowSeconds, completedRetentionSeconds, 900 seconds)`, purged on its own
watermark. A message can outlive its hash row: a late ack then comes back `unresolvable` and
the message redelivers. A hash row can outlive its message, harmlessly. If consumers can be
down longer than an hour, raise `dedupWindowSeconds` deliberately rather than discovering
the boundary through rejected acks. Reporting is on
[Acknowledgement is an offset commit](/use/concepts/ack).

## What you can see afterwards

Each deleting step records what it removed, under which rule (`retention`,
`completed_retention` or `max_wait_time_eviction`) and how many offsets it dropped. The
dashboard's retention view reads those rows, so a queue with retention configured but
nothing to delete shows nothing rather than an error.
