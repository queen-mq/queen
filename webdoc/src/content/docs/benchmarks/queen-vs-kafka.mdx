We ran QueenMQ 2.4, Kafka 4.3.1, Pulsar 4.2.4 and Redpanda 26.2.3 on the same three GCP brokers with
local NVMe disks, with the same load generators, the same load and the same report. At 3,000,000
msg/s of 256-byte messages Kafka has the lowest latency up to 1,000 partitions: a median near 6 ms
and a p99 of 7.5 to 11 ms. QueenMQ 2.4 reads a p99 of 15 to 25 ms at every partition count from 10 to
10,000,000, on 21 to 28 cores. Pulsar reads 20 to 25 ms up to 50,000 partitions on the fewest
cores, and Redpanda 14 ms at 100 and 1,000 partitions and 782 ms at 10,000. Kafka carried 25,000
partitions at a p99 of 105 ms; Kafka and Redpanda did not carry 50,000, and Pulsar did not carry
100,000, so from 100,000 partitions up only QueenMQ carried the rate.

QueenMQ, Pulsar and Redpanda answer a write once two of the three nodes have fsynced it. Kafka, as we
ran it, answers when its replicas hold the write in memory; with an fsync on every append its p99
is 198 ms at 1,000 partitions and 281 ms at 10,000
([the runs](#an-fsync-before-the-answer)). With 1 KB messages at 1,000,000 msg/s QueenMQ's median is
the lower one from 100 partitions up, and Kafka keeps the lower p99 up to 1,000. On one topic of
1,000 partitions Kafka carried about 8,000,000 msg/s and one QueenMQ queue 5,000,000; at 5,000,000
Kafka read a p99 of 8.9 ms, Redpanda 82, QueenMQ 91, and Pulsar, its disks full, did not carry the
rate ([all four at 5,000,000](#all-four-at-5000000-msgs)).

QueenMQ 2.4 writes 147 bytes a message where 2.3.0 wrote 248, about what Kafka writes for this load;
the queue log's fsync went from about 3 ms to 0.5, and the p99 of the same matrix fell from 42 to
76 ms on 2.3.0 to 15 to 25. The 2.3.0 tables of this page are in its git history.

## The machines

GCP VMs in one zone (`europe-west1-b`) and one VPC, Ubuntu 24.04. Every system ran on three
brokers of the same machine type, one system at a time, with nothing else on them.

| Role | Machine type | CPU | Memory | Disk for the data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 brokers | `c4-standard-24-lssd` | 24 vCPU, Intel Xeon 6985P-C | 88 GB | the four local NVMe disks of the machine type (4 × 375 GB) in one RAID0, XFS `noatime` |
| 3 load generators (6 for Pulsar at 50,000 and 100,000 partitions and at 5,000,000 msg/s) | `c4-highcpu-16` | 16 vCPU, Intel Xeon Platinum 8581C | 32 GB | boot disk only |

The runs come from three sets of these VMs, made on 2026-10-14 (Kafka), 10-15 (QueenMQ 2.4) and 10-16
(Pulsar, Redpanda, and QueenMQ at 1,000 and 50,000 partitions). We measured every broker's disks
before each set was used, because one slow disk on a follower becomes the p99 of every client of
that follower: on every broker of every set a 32 KB write followed by `fdatasync` took 0.05 to
0.06 ms at the median, 16,000 to 17,700 a second; on the first set sequential 1 MB writes ran at
1,340 MB/s, GCP's limit of about 335 to 350 MB/s for each local disk, four times. On 10-16 we ran QueenMQ
and Kafka again at 3,000,000 msg/s on 1,000 partitions: QueenMQ read 6.9 / 16.5 ms (four of its five
runs on 10-15 read a p99 of 15 to 20 ms) and Kafka 6.0 / 7.9 ms (8.1 on 10-14), so the three sets
compare.

## How we ran it

- **The load.** Nine load processes, three on each load generator, open loop: each process keeps
  its schedule when the broker slows down, so a stall shows up as latency instead of lowering the
  load. Producers send 100 messages a request to one partition, in turn over all the partitions.
  Kafka and Redpanda share one load generator (`kload`, on franz-go); Pulsar's (`pload`) uses
  pulsar-client-go and QueenMQ's (`qload`) QueenMQ's HTTP API, on the same core of pacing, payloads and
  histograms. Each QueenMQ process talks to one of the three brokers; the other clients talk to each
  partition's leader.
- **Pulsar at 50,000 and 100,000 partitions and at 5,000,000 msg/s** ran on six load generators,
  18 processes. On three,
  the Go client used all 16 cores of each load generator at 50,000 partitions, about 2.2 to 2.7
  cores for every 100,000 msg/s against 0.7 at 10,000, and the run measured the clients: 1,680,000
  msg/s pushed with the brokers at 13 of 72 cores. On six it carried the rate.
- **The messages.** JSON events of 256 bytes, or about 1,027 bytes in the 1 KB runs, the same
  generator for every system.
- **Consumers.** One consumer group or subscription. 9 consumers in all for 10 partitions, 99 for
  100 and 297 for every larger count (306 for Pulsar on six load generators). QueenMQ's consumers pop
  up to 10 partitions and 1,000 messages at a time from 1,000 partitions up (one partition and up
  to 20,000 or 5,000 messages with 10 and 100) and acknowledge without waiting; Kafka's and
  Redpanda's poll up to 1,000 records; Pulsar's failover subscription gives each partition to one
  consumer, which acknowledges every message.
- **A point.** A fresh cluster, the queue or topic created with all its partitions and warmed
  before the timed run, then 70 seconds of load, the first 10 a ramp (90 seconds for QueenMQ's 10-16
  runs). No system was measured while it created partitions. Pulsar's warm-up sends one message to
  every partition from one process; at 100,000 partitions it ran for more than 25 minutes, so that
  point ran without it, its producers created before the start.
- **Latency.** End to end, from the moment a message was scheduled to be sent to the moment a
  consumer received it. Each load process keeps a histogram for every 10-second window; for each
  window we take the slowest process. The tables give the median of the three windows in seconds
  30 to 60 and, in brackets, the worst of them (the statistic of our other benchmark pages). The
  figures use the median: one spike in one window is shown in the brackets and does not decide the
  line.
- **Carried.** A point is carried when at least 99.5% of the offered rate is pushed, nothing is
  shed or errs, and at least 99% of what was pushed is consumed.
- **Resources.** CPU is the three brokers' cores added up over the loaded window. Redpanda's is its
  shards' busy time: its reactors poll, so the operating system counts 66 to 71 of the 72 cores at
  every point. Memory is the resident memory of the busiest broker, disk the mean write rate of a
  broker's data volume. Each broker and load generator was sampled every one or two seconds.
- **One run a point.** We ran most points once. Repeats on the same machines moved QueenMQ's p99 by
  a few milliseconds, and one of six runs of its 1,000-partition point read 65 ms (below).

Up to 1,000 partitions Kafka has the lowest latency: a median of 6.0 to 6.4 ms and a p99 of 7.5 to
11 ms, steady within about a millisecond across the windows at 100 and 1,000, on 15 to 21 cores. It
answers a write when the replicas hold it in memory, where the other three wait for two fsyncs.
From 10,000 partitions Kafka pays for every partition: at 10,000 its p99 is level with QueenMQ's and
Pulsar's (21 ms against 20) on 53 cores, more than twice theirs, and at 25,000 it reads 58 / 105 ms
on 57 cores. At 50,000 and 100,000 it pushed 601,000 and 174,000 of the 3,000,000 messages a second
offered.

QueenMQ's latency, CPU and disk do not follow the partition count: a median of 6.9 to 10 ms and a p99
of 15 to 25 ms from 10 to 10,000,000 partitions, on 21 to 28 cores. The leader held 5 to 8 GB of
memory up to a million partitions and 17 GB at ten million, and each broker wrote 138 to 186 bytes
a message, 440 MB/s at 1,000 partitions where a Kafka broker wrote 443. Its 1,000-partition point
ran six times on two days: a p99 of 15, 16.5, 17, 18 and 20 ms, and once 65 ms with the median at
12, in a run where the log's group commit settled on fewer, larger groups from the first seconds;
why, we did not find.

Pulsar uses the fewest cores at every count it carried, 5 to 22, and its p99 stays between 20 and
25 ms up to 50,000 partitions. Its median is 13 ms at 10 and 100 partitions and 6.2 to 6.7 ms from
1,000 up. At 100,000 partitions it pushed 2,520,000 msg/s with its brokers at 23 of 72 cores and
the six load generators at about 13 of their 16; these runs do not tell whether the brokers or the
client stopped it. Each broker wrote 860 to 900 MB/s, every entry going to a bookie's journal and
then to its ledger storage.

Redpanda reads 14 ms at 100 and 1,000 partitions, 44 ms at 10, and 68 / 782 ms at 10,000, where it
still pushed the rate. A partition is a raft group served by one core: at 10 partitions each one
carries 300,000 msg/s, and the brokers' own produce latency (Redpanda's request histogram, in
buckets that double) had its p99 in the bucket up to 33 ms against up to 8 ms at 100 partitions.
At 10,000 partitions the brokers' produce p99 rose to the bucket up to 262 ms on two brokers and up
to 1 s on the third, whose disks showed four times the I/O pressure of
the others at the same write rate; at 10, 100 and 1,000 partitions the three were alike. The cause
was not determined. At 50,000 Redpanda pushed 2,820,000 of the 3,000,000 and fell behind. Its
disks took about 85,000 writes a second of 7.5 KB each, 640 to 680 MB/s a broker, at every
partition count.

## An fsync before the answer

QueenMQ, Pulsar and Redpanda answer a write after two nodes have fsynced it; Kafka above answers when
its replicas hold it in memory. To see what durability costs Kafka we ran it once more with
`log.flush.interval.messages=1`, an fsync of every append on the leader and on the followers before
it counts, everything else as above. At 3,000,000 msg/s, p50 / p99 in milliseconds (worst window in
brackets) and the cores of the three brokers:

| Partitions | Kafka, fsync on every append | Redpanda | Pulsar | QueenMQ 2.4 | Kafka, no fsync |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 7.5 / 12 (13), 29.3 | 6.9 / 14 (14), 19.9 | 13 / 25 (26), 11.4 | 7.5 / 17 (17), 27.7 | 6.0 / 7.5 (8), 18.5 |
| 1,000 | 7.6 / 198 (285), 30.2 | 6.8 / 14 (20), 22.4 | 6.2 / 20 (21), 15.1 | 6.9 / 16.5 (19), 24.6 | 6.0 / 8.1 (8), 21.0 |
| 10,000 | 95 / 281 (301), 39.1 | 68 / 782 (815), 30.7 | 6.7 / 20 (21), 17.2 | 7.1 / 20 (26), 24.8 | 8.4 / 21 (78), 52.8 |

With few partitions durability costs Kafka little: 1.5 ms on the median and 4 ms on the p99 at 100
partitions, where it is still the fastest. The cost grows with the partition count, because Kafka
syncs a file per partition: at 1,000 partitions its p99 swung from 89 to 440 ms over the first 50
seconds and settled at 12 ms in the last two windows, and at 10,000 it read 95 / 281 ms. Redpanda,
which fsyncs every write by default, reads 14 ms at 1,000 partitions, fourteen times under Kafka
with an fsync, and 782 ms at 10,000 against Kafka's 281. Pulsar and QueenMQ read 20 ms at 10,000:
a bookie's journal takes the entries of all its ledgers, and QueenMQ's log those of all the
partitions of a raft group, so more partitions do not mean more fsyncs. With 1 KB messages at 1,000,000 msg/s on 1,000
partitions, Kafka with an fsync read 8.1 / 11 ms on 15.9 cores against 7.4 / 9.4 without, and QueenMQ
6.8 / 16. Kafka's disks wrote about twice the bytes with the fsync (978 against 435 MB/s a broker at
100 partitions): a page that is still filling is written again on every flush. Each Kafka point ran
once, and its ceiling with an fsync on every append was not measured.

## Kafka in queue mode

Every Kafka number above uses a consumer group: each partition goes to one consumer, which commits
one offset for everything it has read. QueenMQ keeps a lease and an ack for every message. Kafka has
a mode that does the same: a share group ([KIP-932](https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/display/KAFKA/KIP-932%3A+Queues+for+Kafka)),
where the partition's leader locks each record for the consumer that fetched it (30 s by
default), takes an ack per record, redelivers what is released or times out, and writes that state
to an internal topic through a share coordinator. Several consumers read one partition at once, so
a share group keeps no order within a partition; QueenMQ gives a partition to one worker at a time.
We ran it at 3,000,000 msg/s with the same 297 consumers as the consumer-group runs, which needs
`group.share.max.size` raised from its default of 200 to 300. Each consumer accepts every record it
polls, and the acks travel with its next fetch. p50 / p99 in milliseconds (worst window in
brackets) and the cores of the three brokers:

| Partitions | Kafka, share group | cores | Kafka, consumer group | cores | QueenMQ 2.4 | cores |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 6.3 / 9.3 (9) | 24.6 | 6.0 / 7.5 (8) | 18.5 | 7.5 / 17 (17) | 27.7 |
| 1,000 | 6.4 / 23 (23) | 35.0 | 6.0 / 8.1 (8) | 21.0 | 6.9 / 16.5 (19) | 24.6 |
| 10,000 | 78 / 163 (206) | 52.2 | 8.4 / 21 (78) | 52.8 | 7.1 / 20 (26) | 24.8 |

## Kafka and the filesystem

Our first Kafka runs used the brokers' ext4 as it came, with `noatime`, and our hosts' background
writeback from 5% of memory: on 88 GB brokers about 3.6 GB of dirty pages, written out at the
RAID's limit about every 12 seconds. Kafka's p99 came in bursts. At 3,000,000 msg/s it read 938 ms
at 10 partitions and 43 to 96 ms from 100 to 10,000 in the median window, with worst windows up to
1,401 ms; at 2,000,000 msg/s on 1,000 partitions it read 71 and 95 ms in the worst window of two
runs, against 25 ms on the 16-vCPU DigitalOcean machines on 2026-10-13. In every 10-second
window all nine load processes on all three load generators read the same p99, and the producers'
p99 equalled the end-to-end p99: a stall on the write path of the whole cluster at once, with the
brokers at 4 of 24 cores.

A sampler listed every 20 ms the Kafka threads blocked in the kernel. Request handlers and replica
fetchers stopped for up to 300 ms at a time, nearly always in the same place: a `write()` updating
the file's modification time (`ext4_dirty_inode`) waited for write access to an inode block that
the journal was committing (`jbd2_journal_get_write_access`), and the commit waited behind the
page cache writeback queued on the local disks, 50 to 190 ms a write in the worst seconds. So
every append could wait for a journal commit, and the commit for the disks.

We changed one thing at a time, a fresh cluster for every run. Background writeback from 1 GiB
writes the page cache out in smaller steps; it lowered the worst windows but not every median.
Mounting ext4 with `lazytime` keeps timestamp updates in memory, so an append no longer touches
the journal: it removed most of the stall (2,000,000 msg/s on 1,000 partitions went to 6.3 / 9.9
ms in the worst window). XFS, with the thread pools sized for 24 cores, removed the spikes that
were left. We changed the filesystem and the thread pools together, so these runs do not split
that last step between them. Kafka's p99 at 3,000,000 msg/s, the median of the three windows
(worst window in brackets):

| Partitions | ext4, writeback from 5% | ext4, from 1 GiB | ext4 `lazytime`, 1 GiB | XFS, 1 GiB, threads for 24 vCPU |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 938 (1,401) | 506 (514) | 272 (350) | 11 (45) |
| 100 | 96 (103) | 50 (61) | 13 (15) | 7.5 (8) |
| 1,000 | 43 (148) | 88 (104) | 15 (29) | 8.1 (8) |
| 10,000 | 46 (138) | 61 (110) | 34 (46) | 21 (78) |

<Figure id="benchmarks/gcp-kafka-writeback" />

If you run Kafka on machines like these, put its log directories on XFS, as Kafka's documentation
recommends, or at least mount ext4 with `lazytime`, and bound background writeback
(`vm.dirty_background_bytes`) so the page cache goes to disk in small steps. That is why every
system on this page ran on XFS.

## Where each one leads

Kafka, run as on this page, leads on latency up to 1,000 partitions, a median near 6 ms and a p99
of 7.5 to 11 ms at 3,000,000 msg/s, and on throughput, about 8,000,000 msg/s on one topic of 1,000
partitions where one QueenMQ queue carried 5,000,000. It answers from memory: with an fsync on every
append it keeps that lead at 100 partitions and loses it at 1,000 (198 ms) and 10,000 (281 ms).
From 10,000 partitions it needs twice the CPU of QueenMQ or Pulsar, and it did not carry 50,000.

QueenMQ leads on the partition count. It is the one system that carried 3,000,000 msg/s at 100,000
partitions and more, with the same latency, a p99 of 15 to 25 ms, and the same 21 to 28 cores
from 10 partitions to ten million, an fsync on two nodes before every answer. With 1 KB messages
its median is under Kafka's from 100 partitions up, and at 10,000 its p99 too.

Pulsar uses the least CPU wherever it carried the load and holds a p99 of 20 to 25 ms up to 50,000
partitions, with the same two fsyncs before the answer; its Go client needed six load generators at
50,000 partitions, and at 100,000 it did not carry the rate. Redpanda, which fsyncs every write,
reads 14 ms at 100 and 1,000 partitions, against 198 ms for Kafka with an fsync at 1,000, and falls
behind from 10,000.

## What these runs do not show

- Pulsar and Redpanda with 1 KB messages or in transactions, and their ceilings: both ran at
  5,000,000 msg/s on 1,000 partitions only, and Pulsar with its journal sharing the RAID.
- Redpanda with write caching on, where it answers from memory as Kafka does here; Redpanda at
  100,000 partitions.
- Pulsar at 100,000 partitions with enough load generators to rule its client out.
- Reads from the disks. Every consumer read the newest messages, which the brokers still held in
  memory; consumers catching up on data older than what fits in 88 GB were not run.
- Kafka's ceiling with an fsync on every append, and repeats of its fsync points (one run each).
- Kafka on XFS with the thread pools of 16-vCPU brokers, or ext4 with the larger pools: we changed
  the filesystem and the thread pools together.
- Repeated runs for every point: most points ran once, on three sets of VMs of the same type.
- Kafka at 1,000,000 and 10,000,000 partitions, Pulsar and Redpanda past 100,000, QueenMQ at 25,000.
- Kafka's share group at 10 partitions, above 10,000, with 1 KB messages, or at its ceiling, and
  with consumers that release or reject records.
