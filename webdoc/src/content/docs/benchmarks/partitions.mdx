This is the test Queen was built for. Give every entity its own partition and your partition
count is your entity count: a million customers, ten million orders. On three GCP nodes with 24
vCPU and local NVMe disks, Queen 2.4 carried 3,000,000 messages a second of 256 bytes in and out
of one queue at every partition count from 10 to 10,000,000, with an end-to-end p99 of 15 to 25
ms on 21 to 28 cores, and 1,000,000 messages a second of 1 KB at 16 to 22 ms. On the same machines
Kafka was the fastest up to 1,000 partitions and did not carry 50,000, Redpanda read 782 ms at
10,000 and did not carry 50,000, and Pulsar held 20 to 25 ms up to 50,000 and did not carry
100,000 (2026-10-14 to 16). [Compare](/introduction/compare/) explains how each system gets order per
entity.

## The shape

The load of [Queen vs Kafka, Pulsar and Redpanda on NVMe](/benchmarks/queen-vs-kafka/#how-we-ran-it),
where the machines and every setting are: 256-byte JSON messages pushed 100 at a time to one
partition by nine open-loop load processes, a fresh cluster for every point, 70 seconds of load
(90 for Queen's runs of 2026-10-16 at 1,000 and 50,000 partitions). A latency is the median of the
three 10-second windows in seconds 30 to 60, each window the slowest load process, with the worst
window's p99 in brackets. Every partition existed before the timed run: Queen's load generator
pushed one message into each partition and drained it, and the other systems' topics were created
and warmed first. No system was measured while it created partitions.

Queen ran as 2.4.0-beta.1 with the binary push, compressed groups, framed pops and counted acks,
deduplication off and nothing deleted during a point; 9 consumers read 10 partitions, 99 read 100,
and 297 read every larger count with pops of up to 10 partitions and 1,000 messages. The
ten-million column ran with `QUEEN_CONSUME_IDLE_UNLOAD_S=30`. Every point was carried: nothing
shed, no error, and at least 99% of what was pushed consumed.

From 100 partitions to ten million the median stays between 6.9 and 8.9 ms and the p99 between
15 and 25 ms, on about 13 cores of the leader and 6 of each follower. Ten partitions are the slowest
row: a partition goes to one consumer at a time, so nine consumers take more than 300,000 messages
a second each. The 1,000- and 50,000-partition rows ran 90 seconds instead of 70, so their nodes
held 20 seconds more of messages' rows (nothing is deleted during a point). Six runs of the
1,000-partition point on two days read a p99 of 15, 16.5, 17, 18 and 20 ms, and once 65 ms, in a
run where the log's group commit settled on fewer, larger groups from the first seconds; why, we
did not find. A broker wrote 138 to 187 bytes for each message; Kafka's wrote 147 at 1,000
partitions.

10,000, on 31 cores against Queen's 17, and did not carry 50,000.

<Figure id="benchmarks/partitions-cpu" />

Kafka is the fastest up to 1,000 partitions, where it answers a write from memory and the others
wait for two fsyncs. From there its CPU follows the count: 21 cores at 1,000, 53 at 10,000 for a
p99 of 21 ms, 57 at 25,000 for 105 ms, and at 50,000 and 100,000 it pushed 601,000 and 174,000 of
the 3,000,000 messages a second. Its topic of 100,000 partitions took about four minutes to create.
Redpanda reads 14 ms at 100 and 1,000 partitions and 782 ms at 10,000, and fell behind at 50,000.
Pulsar uses the fewest cores and holds 20 to 25 ms up to 50,000 partitions; at 50,000 and 100,000
its Go client needed six load generators instead of three, and at 100,000 it pushed 2,520,000 of
the 3,000,000 with its brokers at 23 of 72 cores and the load generators at about 13 of their 16,
so these runs do not tell whether its brokers or its client stopped it. None of the three was run
past 100,000 partitions.

## What a partition costs

Memory is the one thing in Queen that follows the partition count. From 10 partitions to ten
million the leader went from 5.2 to 17.2 GB and a follower from 4.8 to 14.2: about 1.2 KB per
partition on the leader and 0.9 KB on a follower, with 1 KB messages the same. The leader holds a
little more because it also keeps each consumer group's state for the partitions it has read, for
`QUEEN_CONSUME_IDLE_UNLOAD_S` after their last use (600 s by default; the ten-million column ran
with 30 s). Up to 100,000 partitions the difference is under a gigabyte. Memory is what sets the
partition ceiling: ten million partitions took 17 GB of a node's 88.

## Why the line stays flat

In Queen a partition is a few rows in the store, which every node keeps in memory: the partition
itself, its offsets, and a cursor for each consumer group that reads it. Its messages are records
in its queue's log, beside every other partition's, and one fsync covers everything a group of
entries wrote to that log. So a new partition adds memory and nothing else, neither a file nor a
replication stream nor an fsync of its own, and the leader's CPU at ten million partitions is what
it was at a thousand.

Kafka keeps a log of its own for every partition on every replica, with its own files and its own
place in replication; Redpanda runs a raft group per partition, each served by one core; Pulsar
keeps a managed ledger per partition in BookKeeper, though a bookie writes one journal for all of
them. That machinery is cheap with a few hundred partitions and grows with every one you add, which
is what Kafka's cores and Redpanda's latency show from 10,000 on.

The line was flat before 2.4 too, and higher. On these machines 2.3.0 read a p99 of 42 to 76 ms at
3,000,000 msg/s from 10 to ten million partitions; 2.4 writes 147 bytes a message where 2.3.0
wrote 248, its log's fsync takes about 0.5 ms instead of 3, and the p99 fell to 15 to 25 ms. 2.3.0
had removed the costs that showed at high counts: the leader's scan for idle consumer state, which
held 625,000 partitions under one lock; a table of partition names that hashed every name again
each time it doubled; and the merge of the queue log's directory, synced in one go.

## What this page does not show

- Traffic on every partition at once. Each push carried 100 messages for one partition, so 30,000
  pushes a second reached at most 1,800,000 partitions in a minute: all ten million were created,
  written and drained in the prefill, and the timed load touched a part of them. Traffic on all of
  them within a minute means pushes of a message or two per partition, which costs more per message
  and was not run on 2.4.
- Other rates. Every count ran at 3,000,000 msg/s with 256-byte messages and at 1,000,000 with 1 KB;
  the ceiling of one queue was measured on 1,000 partitions only
  ([5,000,000 msg/s](/benchmarks/queen-vs-kafka/#the-ceiling-at-1000-partitions)).
- Deduplication and retention on, many queues instead of one, and keys hashed onto a fixed set of
  partitions: measured on 2.3.0 and 2.0.0 betas, not on 2.4; those tables are in the git history of
  this page.
- Long runs. Each point ran 70 or 90 seconds; the long runs are on
  [soak and failover](/benchmarks/soak/), on an older build.
- Kafka, Pulsar and Redpanda past 100,000 partitions, Redpanda at 100,000, and Pulsar at 100,000
  with enough load generators to rule its client out.
