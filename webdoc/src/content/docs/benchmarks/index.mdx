Queen 2.0 replicates every write through raft, so we measured it as a cluster: three nodes, every
write fsynced on two of them before the client hears back, and a load generator that keeps its
schedule when the broker slows down, so a stall shows up as latency instead of quietly lowering
the load. Then we ran Kafka, Redpanda and Pulsar on the same six machines with the same load and
the same report. These pages hold what came out, each number with the conditions that make it
true, including the places where another system did better.

## Where Queen leads, and where it does not

Queen hardly notices the partition count. A partition costs the leader about 2 KB of memory, and
its messages go into its queue's log next to every other partition's, so ten million partitions
are written and fsynced the way two hundred are. We are proudest of this result, because it is the
reason Queen exists: one partition per entity, as many entities as you have. Kafka, Redpanda and
Pulsar give each partition a log and a replication state of its own, and on these machines Kafka's
p99 went from 18 ms at 200 partitions to 1.5 s at 50,000 before it fell behind at 100,000, while
Pulsar and Redpanda fell behind from 50,000. Queen ran the same 1M msg/s over ten million
partitions with a p99 of 122 ms.

With few partitions, the others are faster. At 1M msg/s on 200 partitions Kafka delivered with an
e2e p99 of 18 ms and Pulsar with 21 ms, against Queen's 146 ms, and Kafka used a little over half
of Queen's CPU. Kafka went on to 4M msg/s at a p99 of 75 ms; Queen's single raft leader tops out
near 1.5M. Durability explains part of the gap to Kafka, which answered from the page cache, but
none of the gap to Pulsar, which waited for two fsyncs as Queen did. Most of what remains comes from
two choices we made: a Queen pop is a write too, because the lease it hands out commits through the
log before the consumer sees a message, and one leader orders every write of a raft group.

Transactions are where that design pays. A step's acks and pushes are one log entry, so a
transaction costs about one write: 3 ms at the median, and 9,000 single-message transactions a
second went through where Kafka, Redpanda and Pulsar all fell behind. The ceiling is the one thread
that plans them. At 200 partitions Queen and Pulsar top out near the same 7,000 transactions a
second; with 900 partitions and 900 workers Pulsar doubled to 14,400 and Queen dropped to 5,100.

## The rig, in short

Six DigitalOcean droplets with 16 vCPU and 31 GB each, in one VPC: three brokers and three load
machines, plus a fourth load machine for Queen's native runs, because its HTTP load generator needs
more CPU per message than the Kafka and Pulsar clients. Two brokers have a Xeon Gold 6548N and the
third an older Xeon Platinum 8358, which we kept from leading Queen's raft group; Kafka, Redpanda
and Pulsar spread their partition leaders over all three. Each system ran alone on the brokers,
with the same OS settings.

An e2e latency is measured per message, from the instant its producer was scheduled to send it to
the instant a consumer received it, before the ack. Unless a page says otherwise, a latency is the
worst of the load processes over the last 30 seconds of a 60-second run, and a system "fell behind"
when it consumed less than 97% of the offered rate or its e2e p99 passed 5 seconds. Every number
names its build and date, and the engine moves fast: read a figure as what that build did on this
rig. The full method, each system's settings and the commands to run it again are in
[how we measure](/benchmarks/methodology/).

Results measured on Queen 1.x, whose storage was PostgreSQL, stay in the git history of this site.
They are not 2.0 numbers.
