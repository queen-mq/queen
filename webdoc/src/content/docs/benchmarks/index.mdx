Queen replicates every write through raft, so we measure it as a cluster: three nodes, every
write fsynced on two of them before the client hears back, and a load generator that keeps its
schedule when the broker slows down, so a stall shows up as latency instead of quietly lowering
the load. Then we run the other systems on the same machines with the same load and the same
report. These pages hold what came out, each number with the conditions that make it true,
including the places where another system did better.

Queen 2.4, in its first beta, ran against Kafka, Pulsar and Redpanda on three GCP nodes with 24
vCPU and local NVMe disks on 2026-10-14 to 16
([Queen vs Kafka, Pulsar and Redpanda on NVMe](/benchmarks/queen-vs-kafka/)); the released 2.3.0
ran there against Kafka on 10-14, and its tables are in the git history of that page. The partition
count page holds the same runs. The pages on transactions and Kafka clients hold the runs of
2026-10-12 and 13, on pre-release builds of 2.3.0 and three DigitalOcean nodes with 16 vCPU, and
September's runs of Kafka, Redpanda and Pulsar in transactions against Queen 2.0.0-beta.2 on other
16-vCPU machines. Every table names its machines and build, and no number is compared across two
kinds of machines.

## Where Queen leads, and where it does not

Queen hardly notices the partition count. A partition costs every node about 1 KB of memory, and
its messages go into its queue's log next to every other partition's, so ten million partitions
are written and fsynced the way ten are. We are proudest of this result, because it is the reason
Queen exists: one partition per entity, as many entities as you have. Kafka gives each partition
a log and a replication state of its own. On the GCP nodes at 3,000,000 msg/s it used 53 cores
at 10,000 partitions against Queen's 25, and 57 at 25,000 for a p99 of 105 ms; at 50,000 and
100,000 it pushed 601,000 and 174,000 of the 3,000,000 messages a second offered. Redpanda, a raft
group per partition, read 782 ms at 10,000 and fell behind at 50,000; Pulsar held 20 to 25 ms up
to 50,000 and fell behind at 100,000. Queen ran the same load over ten million partitions with a
p99 of 25 ms on 25 cores.

Up to 1,000 partitions Kafka answers sooner and goes further. At 3,000,000 msg/s its median was
about 6 ms and its p99 7.5 to 11 ms, where Queen 2.4's were 6.9 to 10 ms and 16.5 to 24, and up to
1,000 partitions it used less CPU (15 to 21 cores against Queen's 21 to 28). On one topic of 1,000
partitions Kafka carried about 8,000,000 msg/s at a p99 near 12 ms; one Queen queue carried
5,000,000 and not 5,500,000, with neither the disks nor the leader's CPU full, and what stops it
there is not profiled yet. Kafka answers from memory: with an fsync on every append it still read
7.5 / 12 ms at 100 partitions, but 198 ms at 1,000 and 281 at 10,000, where Queen read 16.5 and 20
([fsync runs](/benchmarks/queen-vs-kafka/#an-fsync-before-the-answer)). The rest of its lead comes
from Queen's design: a Queen pop is a write too, because the lease it hands out commits through
the log before the consumer sees a message, and one leader orders every write of a raft group.

Transactions are where that design pays. A step's acks and pushes are one log entry, so a
transaction costs about one write: 3 to 4 ms at the median. In September's comparison 9,000
single-message transactions a second went through where Kafka, Redpanda and Pulsar all fell
behind. The ceiling is the one thread that plans them: near 12,900 transactions of 10 messages a
second on the 16-vCPU nodes, and more workers do not raise it; in September Pulsar doubled its
rate with 900 partitions and 900 workers where Queen's fell.

## The rig, in short

Queen 2.4 ran against Kafka, Pulsar and Redpanda on GCP VMs in one zone and one VPC: three
`c4-standard-24-lssd` brokers with 24 vCPU, 88 GB and four local NVMe disks in one RAID0 with XFS,
and three `c4-highcpu-16` load machines, six for Pulsar's largest points. The 2.3.0 runs of
2026-10-12 and 13, the transactions and the Kafka
clients used six DigitalOcean droplets with 16 vCPU and 31 GB each, in one VPC, the three with the
fastest disks as brokers: a Xeon Gold 6548N that leads Queen's raft group and two Xeon Platinum
8358. Kafka spreads its partition leaders over all three brokers. Each system ran alone on the
brokers, with the same OS settings, except page cache writeback on the GCP nodes (from 1 GiB for
Kafka, which its tail needed there, and for Pulsar and Redpanda) and Redpanda's own tuners.

An e2e latency is measured per message, from the instant its producer was scheduled to send it to
the instant a consumer received it, before the ack. In the runs since 2.3.0 each 10-second window takes
the slowest load process, on a fresh cluster; on the DigitalOcean nodes a latency is the worst
window in seconds 30 to 60 of 90, and on the GCP nodes the median of the three windows in seconds
30 to 60 of 70, with the worst in brackets. A rate is carried when nothing was shed, nothing
erred and 99% of what was pushed was consumed; September's runs took the last 30 seconds
of 60 and called a system behind under 97%. Every number names its build and date, and the engine
moves fast: read a figure as what that build did on that rig. The GCP figures are Queen
2.4.0-beta.1; the DigitalOcean ones are the builds of the two days before 2.3.0. Both ran with the
binary wire forms a client turns on; the default JSON forms have not been run through the matrix.
The Jepsen campaign ran on the code of 2.6.0. The full method, each system's settings and the
commands to run it again are in [how we measure](/benchmarks/methodology/), and for the GCP runs
on [Queen vs Kafka, Pulsar and Redpanda](/benchmarks/queen-vs-kafka/#how-we-ran-it).

Results measured on Queen 1.x, whose storage was PostgreSQL, stay in the git history of this site.
They are not 2.x numbers.
