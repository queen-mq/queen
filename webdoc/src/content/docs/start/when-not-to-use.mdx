Queen is built for one shape of work: many entities, each with its own ordered stream of events,
where a worker's step (the ack, the state it changes, the events it emits and the timer it sets)
has to commit whole. Outside that shape another system is often the better choice. Each case below
names it. The side-by-side view of the same systems is on [Compare](/start/compare/).

## You route messages by their content

Queen has no exchanges, bindings, topic patterns or header matching: the producer names the queue
and the partition. When messages have to reach queues the producer does not know about, by type,
by topic pattern or by header, use RabbitMQ. It also speaks AMQP 0-9-1 and 1.0, MQTT, STOMP and its
stream protocol, where Queen speaks HTTP and the Kafka wire protocol.

## You need the most throughput from a few hundred partitions

On 200 partitions, Kafka kept up with 4,000,000 messages a second in our runs and Queen with
1,000,000, with Kafka at a lower p99 and about half the CPU. Pulsar kept up with 2,000,000, the
highest rate we ran for it, and beat Queen on latency and CPU at 200 and at 10,000 partitions.
These runs used three 16-vCPU nodes, 256-byte messages in batches of 100 and Queen 2.0.0-beta.2
([throughput](/benchmarks/throughput/)). Part of Kafka's lead is that it acknowledges a write from
the page cache, before an fsync; Queen and Pulsar wait for two of three fsyncs.

If your keys fit a few hundred partitions and the order you need is per partition rather than per
entity, Kafka or Pulsar is the cheaper broker. Queen's cost stays flat as partitions grow, which is
where it pulls ahead: at 50,000 partitions Kafka's p99 was 1.5 s and Pulsar fell behind, while
Queen's stayed at 103 ms ([partitions](/benchmarks/partitions/)).

## You depend on the Kafka ecosystem

Kafka Connect and its connectors, Kafka Streams and its state stores, schema registries and log
compaction are Kafka's. Queen's [Kafka facade](/guides/kafka/) lets existing producers and
consumers connect unchanged and accepts the topic settings Streams and Connect create, but Queen
does not compact: a compacted topic keeps every record. If compaction or a connector is what your
system stands on, stay on Kafka.

## Your transactions are the bottleneck

With 10 messages per transaction, Pulsar sustained 14,400 transactions a second over 900
partitions (17,600 over 1,800, while falling behind); Queen's best was 8,900, in one raft group. One leader orders every write of a raft
group, so one tenant's write rate is what one leader can plan. Queen scales out with more raft
groups and more clusters, and a single tenant does not span them. A Queen commit was faster at low
rates: 3 ms at p50 and 4 ms at p99 with 10 messages per transaction at 9,000 messages a second,
where Kafka took 12 and 34 ms and Pulsar 22 and 32 ms ([transactions](/benchmarks/transactions/)).

## Your state is relational

Queen's [KV](/concepts/kv/) reads by key or by prefix. When the state a step changes needs SQL,
joins, constraints or ad hoc queries, keep it in PostgreSQL with a transactional outbox, with the
backups and tooling you already run, and accept that the publish and the consumer's ack commit
separately from it.

## You want to write workflows as code

Temporal and Restate are better for authoring long workflows as code, where loops, branches, waits
of days and compensations read as one function. Queen keeps the state machine as data instead: the
partition is its input, KV is its state and one transaction is one transition, so any language that
speaks HTTP can be the worker and nothing replays your code. See
[one state machine per entity](/guides/state-machines/).

## You want nothing to operate

Amazon SQS is better when you want nothing to run: no server to patch or back up, a price per
request, and no partition count to think about. Queen is a binary you run, as one node or as a
cluster of three or five. A hosted Queen exists: [Queen Cloud](https://queenmq.cloud/) has a free
tier.

## One hot entity needs more than one worker

A partition is sequential by design: one entity's events are processed one step at a time, in
order. Parallelism comes from having many partitions with work. If a single entity's load needs
several consumers at once, it is not one partition's work: split the entity, or use a system that
gives up order for parallelism, such as Kafka's share groups.

## Every effect must happen exactly once

Delivery is at-least-once. Effects inside Queen are exactly-once, through
[transactions](/concepts/transactions/) and [`transactionId` dedup](/concepts/dedup/). A call your
worker makes to another system, a payment API or an email provider, is outside any commit and needs
that system's own idempotency key. No broker removes that, Queen included.

## You need interactive transactions

A transaction is one call, inside one tenant: the ack, KV writes, pushes and timers go in one
request. There is no interactive BEGIN and COMMIT, so a transaction cannot read a value and branch
on it. A step that reads, decides and writes reads the key first, then commits with `expect` on the
version it read and `required: true`, and the whole transaction rolls back if another writer got
there first ([transaction](/reference/transaction/)). When a step needs many reads and decisions
inside one isolation boundary, that is a database transaction.

## You need durable in-memory queues

[Ephemeral queues](/guides/ephemeral/) keep their contents in memory, outside the log. A graceful
stop hands them to the next owner, but a node that crashes loses what it held. Use them for
request/reply, presence and signals, and a regular queue for anything that must survive a crash.
